HRSA Methodology · Single Audit Compliance

How HRSA identifies grantees with a delinquent Single Audit

Organizations that expend federal funding above a set threshold must obtain an independent Single Audit each year. HRSA’s process reviews every grantee, one fiscal year at a time, and sorts each into one of five outcomes.

Methodology owner
HRSAHealth Resources & Services Administration
Governing rule
2 CFR Part 200Uniform Guidance — Subpart F

This summarizes an automated screening HRSA uses to prioritize follow‑up — it is not a final compliance determination. Individual results, especially near the funding threshold, should be confirmed before any action.

Assessment: strengths, limitations & assumptions

An independent read of HRSA’s methodology, prepared for this review. The aim is to be clear about what the screening does well and where its results should be confirmed before action is taken. The methodology itself is detailed below.

Bottom line

A sound screening and triage tool — reliable for narrowing a large grantee population down to a prioritized follow‑up list. It is not a final compliance determination: results near the dollar threshold, and cases sent to manual review, should be confirmed individually.

Strengths

  • Checks for a filed audit first. A grantee with an audit on file is treated as compliant regardless of funding — matching how the rule actually works.
  • Recognizes parent‑organization coverage. Grantees audited under a parent entity are correctly credited, which avoids false delinquents.
  • Applies the current thresholds. Includes the increase from $750K to $1M for fiscal years beginning on/after Oct 1, 2024, based on each grantee’s own fiscal year.
  • Uses the correct 9‑month filing deadline from the Uniform Guidance.
  • Fails safe. When funding data is missing or a fiscal year‑end can’t be determined, cases are routed to human review rather than cleared.
  • Repeatable and auditable. Every run is logged and can be re‑run; results update in place rather than duplicate.

Limitations

  • HighHHS funding only. The threshold is based on total federal funding across all agencies, but this screening sees only HHS funding. “No audit required” therefore means “based on HHS funding.”
  • HighAwarded vs. spent. The rule is based on funds spent; this screening uses funds awarded (see the methodology note below). Borderline cases may differ.
  • MedFiscal‑year alignment. Funding is totaled by the federal fiscal year (Oct–Sep). For grantees whose year ends in another month, the funding window is shifted, which can affect borderline cases.
  • MedFinds missing audits, not late ones. A grantee that eventually filed — even well past the deadline — is counted as compliant.
  • MedFirst‑time non‑filers. A grantee’s year‑end is inferred from its past audits, so one that crossed the threshold but has never filed goes to manual review rather than “delinquent.”
  • LowStandard annual audits assumed. Special cases such as biennial or program‑specific audits are not separately handled.

Assumptions to confirm before relying on results

  • HHS funding is a sufficient stand‑in for total federal funding.
  • Funds awarded approximate funds spent.
  • Each grantee’s fiscal year aligns closely enough with the federal fiscal year.
  • The excluded‑program list (93.255 and 93.530) is correct and complete.
  • A filed audit means compliant (timing of filed audits is not evaluated).
  • Grantees follow a standard annual Single Audit schedule.
Methodology note — please read

How “funding” is measured: awarded vs. spent

The audit requirement depends on a dollar threshold. There are two ways to measure a grantee’s federal funding, and they are not always the same number:

What the rule cites

Money spent

The regulation bases the audit requirement on what a grantee actually spends during its fiscal year (its “expenditures”). This amount is self‑reported by the recipient and is not independently available as federal data.

What this screening uses

Money awarded

The screening measures what was awarded to each grantee (its “obligations”), because award amounts are reliably reported per organization while spending amounts often are not.

The two figures can be close, but they can also be very different — money awarded in one year may be spent across several. For grantees near the dollar threshold, measuring awarded funding instead of money spent can place some on the wrong side of the equation — flagging a grantee that was not actually required to audit, or clearing one that was. Results close to the threshold should be treated as a prompt to verify, not a final answer.

What each outcome means

Compliant The required audit is on file — filed by the grantee, or covered through a parent organization. When an audit exists, funding is not even checked. Questions 1–2
No audit required The grantee received less than the funding threshold (after setting aside certain excluded grants), so no Single Audit was required. Question 3
Audit not yet due The grantee crossed the threshold and has no audit on file — but the filing deadline has not arrived yet. Not late, just pending. Question 5
Delinquent An audit was required, the deadline has passed, and none was found anywhere. These are the cases that need follow‑up. Question 5
Needs manual review There was not enough information to decide automatically — missing funding data, or an unknown fiscal year‑end. A person should review these individually. Questions 3–4
